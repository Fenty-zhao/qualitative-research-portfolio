# Making Sense of Challenges While Living Away from Home

**An interview-based qualitative research case study · University of Manchester MSc coursework**

[Portfolio home](../../README.md) · [Coding evidence](coding-to-themes.md) · [Research reflection](reflexivity-EN.md) · [Methods & ethics](methods-and-ethics.md)

## Project overview

**Research question:** *How do students make sense of and respond to the challenges they face when living away from home?*

This question was set as part of my qualitative research methods coursework. I explored it through the experience of a Chinese international Master's student living independently in the UK.

Rather than only cataloguing the difficulties she encountered, I was interested in **what she considered a challenge**, what she did when problems arose, and how she responded to expectations about studying abroad. I conducted a semi-structured interview and analysed the account through qualitative coding and thematic analysis.

## Research process

### 1. Preparing the interview

The question concerned personal interpretations, so I used a semi-structured interview to explore how the participant described her experiences in her own terms. The guide began with her earlier experiences of home and living arrangements, then moved to life in the UK, everyday difficulties, and ways of responding to them.

I approached a classmate because she was accessible, relevant to the topic, and someone I thought would be willing to take part. This was a practical recruitment choice involving a participant who met the study criteria, rather than a systematic selection from a wider pool.

### 2. Following the participant's account

The interview guide provided direction, but some questions needed to change as I learned more about her experiences. I had expected her to have lived away from home before arriving in the UK and had prepared questions comparing her earlier experiences in China with her current life. In fact, she had not previously lived away from home. I adjusted the discussion towards her experience of independent living for the first time.

The interview also developed through clarification and follow-up. For example, when she described “leaving home” as something concrete rather than an emotionally significant separation, I returned to an earlier point in the conversation and invited her to explain what she meant.

Our shared language and cultural references helped the conversation flow, while the differences in our educational backgrounds also made some of my initial expectations visible. I explore both sides of this relationship in the [research reflection](reflexivity-EN.md).

### 3. Analysing the material

I reviewed the Chinese interview transcript and worked through recurring ways the participant described difficulties, decisions, emotions, and social expectations. Manual interpretation was supported by AI assistance in organising codes and developing the written analysis.

The saved Chinese working draft includes codes such as **High Challenge Threshold**, **Normalization of Obstacles**, **De-dramatization**, **Functional Separation**, and **Pragmatic Understanding of Mobility**. The final report developed three themes.

## Key findings

### Theme 1 · Treating difficulties as “just something to deal with”

The participant drew a distinction between everyday problems and what she considered genuine challenges. Small obstacles were often described as things to resolve, rather than events that needed to carry a large emotional meaning.

She used the Chinese phrase **“只是个事儿”** (“just something to deal with”) and, with humour, referred to a television obstacle-course competition when discussing what would really count as a challenge. Major decisions such as pursuing a PhD or finding work also came closer to her understanding of a challenge.

She approached the idea of “leaving home” in similarly practical terms. For her, it could simply describe being physically away from her family, without necessarily implying a dramatic emotional break.

**Interpretation:** The way she defined an experience appeared to shape the emotional weight she attached to it.

### Theme 2 · Task-oriented coping and emotional deferral

When the participant described problems, her accounts often moved quickly towards what she needed to do next. She did not present this as an absence of emotion. Rather, she described dealing with the immediate task first and processing how she felt afterwards.

One example involved a sweater she accidentally damaged while washing it. Although upset, she searched for ways to improve the situation before resting. She also described using to-do lists, including ordinary errands, to turn uncertain situations into manageable actions and recognise progress.

**Interpretation:** Practical action and postponed emotional processing worked together in her account of coping.

### Theme 3 · Responding to social expectations through subjectivity construction

The participant was also concerned with how international students were represented by others. She objected to exaggerated social-media portrayals of studying abroad when they seemed to stand in for the experience of an entire group.

This was not a denial that some students struggle. Her objection was to **being represented by one narrative** that did not reflect her experience.

She likewise questioned dismissive comparisons about the academic ability of international Master's students, using her own reasoning rather than automatically accepting a negative label.

**Interpretation:** She defended her ability to define her own experience and identity in the face of external judgements.

## Connecting the themes

The themes address three related aspects of the same account:

1. **Meaning:** What does she consider a challenge?
2. **Action:** How does she respond when something goes wrong?
3. **Identity:** How does she respond when others define what her experience is supposed to mean?

Together, they suggest that coping involved not only completing everyday tasks, but also deciding how to interpret those tasks and resisting descriptions that did not fit her experience.

## Reflecting on the research

Two experiences were especially useful for examining my interview practice.

**First, my own educational background affected the initial guide.** I grew up in a smaller Chinese city, where boarding during secondary school and moving to another city for higher education were familiar, often expected pathways. I brought that expectation into an interview with another Chinese student. Her experience was different: she had studied in her home city before coming to the UK. I changed the line of questioning when I understood that the planned comparison did not apply.

**Second, I sometimes interrupted while trying to keep the interview on track.** The transcript also shows that I returned to an interrupted point and asked the participant to continue. Reviewing those moments helped me think more carefully about the balance between following a guide and giving someone time to explain an idea.

Shared cultural references and an existing peer relationship also supported a natural conversation. One exchange about whether responses to difficulty might be linked to East Asian cultural expectations led the participant to question that generalisation, opening a discussion rather than simply confirming my suggestion.

## Scope and next steps

The study is based on one participant's account. It cannot show how common these patterns are among international students. The interview also provides limited detail about the participant's physical living environment, and it was not combined with diary or observational data.

If developed further, the research could involve students from different educational and geographical backgrounds, additional forms of data collection, and a more systematic contemporaneous coding record.
