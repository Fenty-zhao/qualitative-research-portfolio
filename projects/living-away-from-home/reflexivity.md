# Researcher Reflexivity: Interview Decisions, Rapport and Assumptions

[← Case study](README.md) · [Coding evidence](coding-to-themes.md) · [Methods & ethics](methods-and-ethics.md)

This reflection expands on the self-evaluation in my submitted qualitative methods coursework, drawing on the interview transcript and working drafts.

## 1. Shared background, different experiences

I interviewed a Chinese Master's student whom I knew through our course. We shared a language, some cultural references, and the experience of studying in the UK. Those similarities helped make the interview accessible and conversational.

They did not mean our earlier educational experiences were the same. I grew up in a smaller Chinese city, where boarding during secondary school and moving elsewhere for higher education were familiar, often expected pathways. This shaped my original guide: I expected that the participant had experienced living away from home before studying in the UK and prepared questions comparing life in China and Britain.

During the interview, I learned that she had remained in her home city throughout her earlier education and had not previously lived away from home. Rather than pursue a comparison that could not answer the question for this participant, I adjusted the discussion towards her first experience of independent living.

This was not a problem caused by failing to collect a full biography in advance. An exploratory interview involves learning about the person as the conversation unfolds. The methodological lesson was to notice when an assumption built into the guide did not fit the account, and change direction accordingly.

## 2. Following a guide while making room for an explanation

My semi-structured guide was useful for keeping the interview focused on the research question. Reviewing the transcript, however, I could identify occasions when I interrupted the participant while she was developing an abstract idea. I was thinking about the remaining questions and how to keep the conversation moving.

One example concerned the meaning of “leaving home.” After interrupting, I returned to the point and asked whether she could finish explaining it. She then developed her distinction between *leaving home* as a concrete, physical action and the larger emotional meaning that others might attach to it.

The episode contains both a limitation in my listening and an attempt to repair the interruption during the interview. For future interviews, I would make better use of pauses and allow the participant to complete an idea before offering my own summary.

## 3. Rapport as a methodological resource

Knowing the participant and sharing Chinese cultural references gave the discussion a familiar starting point. We could talk about family expectations, everyday routines and online expressions without having to explain every reference from scratch. The participant discussed personal frustrations, practical difficulties and views she did not agree with.

That familiarity helped support a relaxed exchange. At the same time, a comfortable conversation alone does not prove that an interpretation is correct. Understanding a shared reference is different from understanding the particular meaning the participant gives it. The interview still required clarification and close attention to what she actually said.

## 4. Interpretation as part of the conversation

During the interview, I raised whether the participant's practical way of addressing difficulties might be connected with broader East Asian cultural expectations. Rather than simply endorsing this explanation, she questioned the generalisation. The discussion continued towards differences between people and how group stereotypes are formed.

I do not treat this exchange as a dramatic mistake or a sudden change of research design. Offering a possible interpretation and hearing the participant qualify it can be part of a semi-structured interview. Its value here was that the participant could respond in her own terms and move the conversation beyond the explanation I introduced.

On reviewing the material, the exchange also reinforced the importance of distinguishing the researcher's interpretation from the participant's account.

## 5. Study limits and future improvements

The project drew on a single interview, without additional observational or diary data. It therefore offered depth in one account rather than a basis for describing international Master's students as a population. More information about the participant's daily environment could also have helped readers judge how the findings might relate to other situations.

For future work, I would recruit participants with different geographical and educational backgrounds, keep fuller dated coding notes, and consider complementary data sources where the research question requires them.
